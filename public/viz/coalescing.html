0Why the timestamp is a parameter

set(key: string, value: string, at: number): void   // `at` is GIVEN, not read

A function that reads the clock itself cannot be tested deterministically: your "types two characters 40ms apart" test becomes "types two characters as fast as this machine happened to run today". Injecting the timestamp is both the better design and the thing that lets you write the test in the room, which is the part the interviewer can see.

"I'm going to take the timestamp as an argument rather than calling Date.now() inside. It makes the coalescing testable, and the caller has the real event time anyway — it's on the DOM event."

That is a cheap, real API-design point, and it takes eight seconds. Take it.

2A merge keeps the older before and the newer after

That is the whole implementation of coalescing — one line, and the reason it works. The merged step still describes a single reversible transition, from where the burst started to where it ended, so undo reverses the burst and redo replays it as one.

Keep the newer before instead and undo lands the user in the middle of their own typing. Keep the older after and redo undoes half the burst. Both bugs pass a test that only checks depth.

Two traps inside those nine lines. A merge must not push a second stack entry and must not clear the redo stack twice — both are harmless-looking and both make depth lie. And canCoalesce refuses a top of length ≠ 1, because merging a keystroke into a batch from the other drill would make one undo step that is half gesture and half transaction.

3What forces a boundary

checkpoint() is the one you must volunteer. Without a forced boundary, a user types, clicks somewhere else, types again inside the window, and one undo reverts both — including the part they thought they had finished. Selection change, blur, pointer-up: the caller knows the gesture ended, and the editor cannot.

4The bound, and what it costs the user

Drop from the oldest end. That is the only defensible direction — the newest steps are the ones a user is about to undo — and it means the history has a floor it cannot get back below.

With a limit, "undo everything" no longer reaches the empty document, and that is not a bug: it is the price of the bound, and the sentence to say is that you are choosing it. What would be a bug is undo past the floor throwing, or the document ending up in a state no sequence of edits could have produced.

The follow-up if there is time: bound by memory, not by count. Ten thousand single-property edits are cheap; ten "paste a 400-layer group" edits are not. If steps carry patches you can weigh them; if they carry snapshots you cannot afford many. This is also the moment to mention O(1)-undo-by-patch versus cloning the document — the drill's other half.

5Say it out loud

"I'll take the timestamp as an argument, so coalescing is deterministic and testable."
"Merge into the previous step only if it's a single op on the same key inside the window, and keep the older before with the newer after so the merged step still reverses the whole burst."
"I want a forced boundary too — checkpoint() — because time alone isn't enough. If the user clicks away and comes back within the window, one undo would revert something they'd finished. Real editors end the run on a gesture boundary and treat time as the fallback."
"Time-based merging also makes undo depend on how fast someone types, which isn't reproducible. I'd say that's the argument for making the gesture the primary boundary rather than the milliseconds."
"The bound drops from the oldest end. That means undo can't always reach the empty document — that's the trade, not a bug, and I'd surface it in the UI by greying the button rather than pretending."
